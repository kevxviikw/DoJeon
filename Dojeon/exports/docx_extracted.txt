01   Summary
DoJeon (도전 — “challenge”) is an app for taking on a genuinely hard goal and actually finishing it — not another habit tracker, and not a motivational-content feed.
A member sets a hard, time-boxed goal with a real deadline — ship an app in 30 days, finish a portfolio before internship season, complete an exam-prep sprint. The app turns that goal into a demanding but realistic plan, then keeps the member moving through daily work sessions inside a small squad of fellow PIC members who are working on their own goals at the same time. Progress is proven with real evidence, not a checkbox, and when reality diverges from the plan, the app replans honestly instead of quietly hiding the gap.
This proposal lays out the concept, how it works, what we deliberately chose not to build, the technical plan, and the timeline — and asks for your confirmation to move forward as PIC&apos;s official A-to-Z submission, distributed internally through TestFlight.
02   The Problem
Most “discipline” apps fail in one of two directions. Pure motivational content — the beast-mentality edits and hustle content this idea started from — is passive: a user gets the same feeling from free YouTube content, with zero commitment and zero plan, and it asks nothing of them. Pure enforcement apps — the ones that scold, lock you out, or shame you on a public leaderboard — put the discipline outside the user, in the software. The moment someone disables it or falls behind once, the discipline goes with it, and it fails the actual goal of building real follow-through.
DoJeon is built to sit between those two failure modes: a real plan, real evidence, and real people who are grinding alongside you — not judging you.
03   The Concept
Instead of tracking endless daily habits, a member launches a time-limited mission toward one concrete, checkable result — not “work on my portfolio,” but “publish three completed case studies.” A vague goal can&apos;t be measured; a checkable one can.
Missions run inside a small squad of three to six people — naturally, fellow PIC members — each pursuing their own individual goal in parallel. Squad is the strong recommendation: it&apos;s where the app gets its edge. But it is not a requirement. A member recruiting a squad, or between squads, still gets the complete plan, work session, evidence, and replanning loop with nothing disabled.
04   How It Works: The Core Loop
The product is one loop, repeated daily: Forge, Push, Proof, and Adjust.
Forge — plan the goal
The member states the goal and its deadline. Claude (Anthropic&apos;s AI) breaks it into a plan with three tiers: a Minimum that&apos;s doable even on the worst day, a Target that keeps the mission on schedule, and a Stretch that&apos;s the actual reach goal. If the scope genuinely doesn&apos;t fit the deadline and the member&apos;s available time, the plan says so directly and offers real tradeoffs — extend the deadline, cut scope, or find more time — rather than confidently generating a schedule that was never realistic.
Push — work, together or alone
This is where the squad concept becomes an actual feature rather than a mood board. A work session is a real, structured activity:
Choose today&apos;s task and its intended output.
Start a timed work session.
Squad members can optionally join with their own tasks — no video call required.
Finish by submitting the output, or recording a blocker instead.
See a short summary of what everyone in the squad accomplished.
Participation is asynchronous by default. With three to six people, schedules won&apos;t line up every day, and an empty “working now” screen shouldn&apos;t feel like the product is dead — the summary always shows the squad&apos;s most recent completed sessions, not just who happens to be online right now.
Proof — evidence, not a checkbox
Each check-in requires proof tied to that day&apos;s task — a photo, a short voice note, or a logged count or duration — posted to the squad feed and labeled self-reported, attached, or squad-reviewed. Nothing is automatically stamped “verified,” and the AI can flag a gap or summarize a submission, but it never acts as an infallible judge of someone&apos;s work.
Privacy is a designed requirement, not an afterthought: completion status is always visible to the squad, but the underlying file is opt-in per submission — a member can share that they finished without sharing the file itself. Joining a live session is opt-in every time. Leaving a squad never requires an explanation, and removing a piece of evidence later deletes the file while preserving the honest record that something was, or wasn&apos;t, submitted — so history can&apos;t be quietly rewritten in either direction.
Adjust — honest replanning
When a member falls behind, the app doesn&apos;t silently move the deadline, and it doesn&apos;t pile on unrealistic catch-up work either. It follows a fixed sequence:
One missed session does not automatically rewrite the mission.
The app first checks whether the existing schedule has room to recover.
If the goal genuinely no longer fits, it presents scope, time, and deadline tradeoffs.
The member approves any material change.
The original commitment and the revision both stay visible in the mission&apos;s history.
Repeated Minimum-only days trigger a full plan review, because the deadline may no longer be realistic.
This is the rule that keeps DoJeon feeling like a genuine commitment rather than a polite way to postpone a goal indefinitely.
05   What the Member Actually Sees
The home screen leads with what&apos;s next, not a streak count: the next deliverable, milestone progress, deadline status, and the next scheduled work session. A full history is one tap away, and it&apos;s always accurate, but it isn&apos;t the first thing a member sees when they open the app.
That history also doesn&apos;t lump every setback into one “miss.” It distinguishes four different things that can happen — work that wasn&apos;t done, work that was done but the evidence wasn&apos;t submitted, a day of rest the member planned in advance, and a session that was cut short by a real technical problem. Treating those as the same event would punish people for things that aren&apos;t actually failures.
06   What We Deliberately Left Out
A few choices matter as much as what&apos;s included, because they define what kind of app this is:
NO INVENTED PENALTIES
There are no financial stakes, forfeits, or punitive consequences in the MVP. A miss is never hidden or softened — it&apos;s reported honestly — but the app doesn&apos;t manufacture punishment on top of it. The pressure comes from a small group of real people showing up together, not from fear of a penalty.
NO LEADERBOARD
A cross-member leaderboard was considered and removed from the roadmap entirely. It would turn finishing into performing for an audience instead of doing the work — exactly what this app is built to avoid.
SOLO ISN&apos;T BROKEN
Squad is the strong recommendation, but a member without a squad yet — or between squads — keeps the complete plan, session, evidence, and replanning loop. Nothing about using DoJeon alone is disabled or watered down.
Updated after further club feedback — the “no invented penalties” and “no leaderboard” positions above are partially superseded within a squad. See the Addendum at the end of this proposal.
07   Naming
The proposed name is DoJeon (도전), Korean for “challenge” — a direct tie to PIC&apos;s own slogan, “Challenge Everything, Fear Nothing.” “Beast Mode” has been the internal development codename through the design process and will keep appearing in engineering documentation until the name is finalized. Before this is locked in, we&apos;ll run an App Store Connect uniqueness check, since the app name has to be globally unique even for club-only TestFlight distribution.
08   Technical Feasibility &amp; Distribution
DoJeon builds directly on the infrastructure from Kunwoo&apos;s earlier PIC project (VoiMo, a voice-note app), which keeps the technical risk low:
Apple Developer Program enrollment and the Xcode project scaffold carry over as-is.
Swift and SwiftUI on iOS 17/18, the same stack already in use.
The voice- and photo-evidence capture reuses the existing recording engine and permissions handling.
Claude&apos;s API generates and adjusts the mission plans, using the same key-proxy pattern already built for VoiMo.
Supabase (Postgres) provides the shared backend squads need to see each other&apos;s sessions and check-ins.
Added for the Addendum below: the same Supabase backend also stores squad leaderboard rankings, check-approval votes, and the goal-similarity index used for solo matching.
Distribution is internal TestFlight testing — PIC members are invited directly to the Apple Developer team, which is free and has no external Beta App Review wait, appropriate for a club-only build.

Addendum: Updates From the Club&apos;s Feedback
The four updates below came out of presenting this proposal and talking it through with the club. They refine the design above — and in two places, they supersede specific positions in Section 6 (“No invented penalties” and “No leaderboard”), which is left exactly as originally written above so there&apos;s an accurate record of what was first proposed.
A. Squad-Scoped Leaderboard
Each squad now has a leaderboard, visible only to that squad&apos;s three to six members — never across squads. It ranks members on consistency and completion within their own mission, not against strangers. This updates the “No leaderboard” position in Section 6: the original concern was a cross-squad leaderboard turning finishing into a public performance for people who don&apos;t know each other. A small, known-squad leaderboard is different enough from that to include — everyone on it is already grinding alongside everyone else.
B. Solo Matching
A member who starts alone can now search for potential squad-mates instead of staying solo by default. Matching is based on similarity in goal type and pace, giving new members a starting point for forming a squad rather than leaving it entirely to chance.
C. Peer-Approved Check-Ins &amp; a Removal Threshold
A daily check is no longer confirmed by self-report alone. Submitted evidence is reviewed by the member&apos;s squad-mates, and a check is confirmed once it clears an approval threshold from the squad — evaluation shifts from the individual to peer confirmation within a small, trusted group, rather than an algorithm or a stranger.
A duration-based limit accompanies this: if a member&apos;s missed days pass a set share of their mission&apos;s total length, they are removed from the squad. This is the one point where Section 6&apos;s original “no invented penalties” position is superseded — the club specifically asked for a mechanism with a real consequence, short of anything financial or shame-based. Solo missions are unaffected, since there&apos;s no squad to vote or to be removed from.

D. Mission End &amp; Data
When a mission&apos;s duration ends, its squad disbands. Five members who took on a 50-day “quit smoking” challenge together finish it together, and each then moves on to a new goal, alone or in a new squad. With member consent, DoJeon collects data on achievement, consistency, and behavior patterns across missions at that point.
This is proposed as the app&apos;s basis for revenue, rather than charging for the core mission experience itself. It&apos;s a hypothesis to test against real usage once members are actually running missions, not a finalized business model.
These four updates are proposed alongside the rest of this document and are part of what we&apos;re asking you to confirm.
